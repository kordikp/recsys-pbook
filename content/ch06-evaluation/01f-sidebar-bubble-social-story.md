It started with one video about sourdough. Maja watched it twice — the folding technique was genuinely clever — and the feed took notes.

By Tuesday, every third post was bread. By Friday, it was *only* bread: starters, crumb shots, a man in Vermont whispering to his dough. Maja liked bread. The feed was not wrong. But something felt off, and it took her a week to name it: **nothing surprised her anymore.**

Her friend Tomas posted about a local election that half the city was arguing about. She never saw it. A band she loved announced a tour. Never saw it. The feed hadn't blocked anything — it had simply ranked ten thousand safer bets above them, every single time. That's the trick of a filter bubble: **it's not a wall, it's a ranking.** Nothing is forbidden; the different just never wins the auction for your attention.

The algorithm was doing exactly what it was trained to do. Every bread video she finished told it *more of this*; every serving of it she accepted made the next non-bread post look riskier to show. Engagement stayed high. The metric dashboard, somewhere in a control room, showed a happy user.

What broke the loop was boring: she searched for something unrelated, followed two accounts on purpose, and force-fed the feed a week of deliberate variety. The bubble didn't pop — bubbles don't pop, they *dilute*.

The unsettling part isn't that feeds narrow. It's that a narrowing feed feels like nothing at all. Silence doesn't notify you.
