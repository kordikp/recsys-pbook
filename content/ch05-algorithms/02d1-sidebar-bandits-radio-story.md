Two a.m., a small radio station. Vera has one job: keep drivers listening until the traffic report.

She knows what works. *Track 4* — the safe one — has never failed her; every time she plays it, the request line stays warm. That's **exploiting**: cashing in on what she already knows.

But tonight there's a padded envelope on the mixing desk — a demo from a local band, unknown, unplayed. If it's good, she owns a hit nobody else has for months. If it's bad, some tired driver somewhere switches stations. That's **exploring**: paying a small, real price for information.

Play only Track 4, and the playlist slowly dies of sameness — she'll never find the next Track 4. Play only demos, and tonight's listeners suffer for tomorrow's playlist. Neither extreme survives a month.

So Vera does what good DJs and good recommenders both do: she **budgets the risk**. The known hit anchors the hour; one unknown slides in at 2:40, when the audience is most forgiving. If phones light up, the demo earns a prime slot tomorrow — the experiment graduates. If silence, the cost was three minutes at 2 a.m., the cheapest slot she has.

Your streaming app runs Vera's shift a million times a night. Most of your queue is Track 4s — safe bets from your history. But every so often there's an envelope: a song with almost no plays, slipped in where a skip costs least. Skip it, and the system quietly eats the loss. Play it twice, and somewhere a new artist's numbers move for the first time.

The dilemma never resolves. It's not a bug to fix — it's a budget to set.
